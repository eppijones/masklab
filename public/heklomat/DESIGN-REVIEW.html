HEKLOMAT · CRITICAL DESIGN REVIEW · 4 OCTOBER 2026

Prove the stitch before building the whole machine.

Retire the gate-wheel mechanism as the main development path. Build a constrained-stitch test platform with retained stitch positions, a controlled metal tool and measured yarn tension.

The dominant risk is loss of control over the yarn and stitch position, not how many parts fit on a printer. Spending time completing the old frame would lock us into its weakest assumption. First establish a repeatable stitch process, then design the geometry that carries it around the hat.

Keep a true-crochet, seamless bucket hat as the final target. A small flat swatch is an experiment toward that target, not a substituted finished product. A panel-and-seam route is an alternative only if acceptable to the owner.

Status: Selected direction for experiments; complete-machine CAD not frozen. The replacement has no released complete CAD, bill of materials, firmware or demonstrated hat yet. This is an engineering decision based on the evidence below, not proof that it is the best possible machine.

Replacement modules

  1. Stitch storage

    An exchangeable bed of paired metal holders; start with only enough positions to prove neighbouring-stitch transfer. Print housing and adjustment fixtures; buy precision yarn-contact elements.

    Still to determine: Exact needle SKU, gauge, stroke, clearance and yarn compatibility.

  2. Working tool

    Separate insertion, rotation and hook-closure functions. Track where each loop sits during each motion. Buy polished metal tool and slider; print noncritical housings.

    Still to determine: Available compound tool or equivalent controlled closure; tool supplier drawing.

  3. Yarn system

    Measure tension, feed yarn and take back slack. Start with one colour and one measured yarn. Buy ceramic guides, sensor, bearings and motor; print guides/housings.

    Still to determine: Required tension range, sensor resolution, feed travel and response time.

  4. Fabric control

    Defined cast-off edges and adjustable take-down keep the work in the working plane. Smooth bought or machined contact surfaces; printed mounts.

    Still to determine: Forces, edge geometry, clearance during transfer and finished-fabric removal.

  5. Motion and frame

    Use supplier-dimensioned rails/stages. Choose axis count from the proven stitch sequence. Metal load paths and guides; printed brackets where tolerances permit.

    Still to determine: No axis count, torque, speed or full frame size is released yet.

  6. Controller and recovery

    Local motion limits and stop chain. Verify transfers before advancing; stop for ambiguous state. Standard controller/driver platform and documented harness.

    Still to determine: Controller capacity, wiring, fault response and recovery tests; no deployable firmware exists yet.

Build sequence and acceptance evidence

  1. Characterise the actual yarn and tool

    Measured yarn/hook dimensions, gauge swatch, available tool drawing and printer profile.

    Pass evidence: A supplier-backed tool and clearances compatible with the intended yarn; no reliance on nominal yarn diameter alone.

  2. Manual retained-stitch cell

    Dimensioned fixture, clamp/holder detail and video of the complete stitch and retention transfer.

    Pass evidence: One true single crochet repeatedly completed with traceable loop ownership; record all failures and forces. Operator performs unmotorised motions.

  3. Powered cell and neighbouring stitches

    All mating parts, wiring, homing, tool closure, tension feedback, firmware and an operation log.

    Pass evidence: At least 50 consecutive stitches as an initial development gate, then multiple rows with no manual loop placement between stitches. This is not production qualification.

  4. Faults and longer runs

    Tests for loop loss, snagging, yarn break, stalled motion, sensor failure and restart.

    Pass evidence: Safe, observable stop with the last verified state retained; sustained run and material-change tests. Report per-operation failure/recovery rates.

  5. Choose and prove the hat transport

    Circular retained-stitch transfer prototype, or separately accepted shaped-panel plan.

    Pass evidence: For seamless hats: complete rounds, round transitions, increases and removal are physically demonstrated before full CAD release.

  6. Full manufacturing release

    Complete native CAD and exchange models, every fastener/shaft/guard, tolerance drawings, purchased SKUs, verified plate profiles, final quote and illustrated assembly/wiring guide.

    Pass evidence: A physical single-colour hat passes the chosen dimensions and stitch checks. Add multicolour operation only after reliable yarn selection and strand control.

Why the existing model cannot be released

The previous verification suite passes 243 checks, but it does not test these manufacturing and process requirements. The new checks confirm actual inaccessible pockets and plastic obstructing nominal through-holes, in addition to validating the exported mesh bytes.

  1. MECH-01 — Platter and hub bolt circles do not meet

    The actual CAD cutters put six platter holes on radius 102 mm and six hub holes on radius 53 mm: 49 mm radial mismatch. The legacy fit checks omit this pair.

    Redesign both around the selected bearing and shared bolt pattern, including a centring register, stack height and fastener engagement.

  2. QTY-01 — The nominal gate quantity is short

    The full comb needs 10 gates and the wheel 8; the old registry supplies 12. At least 18 installed gates are needed, before spares.

    Use 18 installed + 2 spares in the provisional full-machine quote; choose the final throat only after yarn trials.

  3. MECH-02 — Bench comb gate pockets are buried

    Even the upper pocket reaches only Z=12.24 mm inside the 14 mm tall comb. Only the small ejector holes reach the top; an 8 × 2.6 mm gate tongue cannot enter from above.

    Use the new open-top calibration rack for initial measurements. Redesign the working comb sockets before assembly.

  4. KIN-01 — Hook shaft is crosswise to its insertion axis

    The hook shaft is modelled along local Z, then mounted with a 90-degree X rotation. Its direction is 90.0 degrees from the P insertion axis. Translating P therefore sweeps the hook sideways rather than driving the tip along its shaft.

    Redesign hook and collet together around a shared tool centreline; verify the tip path, tool closure and collision clearance through the actual stitch cycle. Do not fake this movement in an animation.

  5. GEOM-01 — Several intended through-holes end inside the solid

    bore() creates a centred cutter, while platter and hub bodies are extruded upward from Z=0. The platter cutter ends at Z=8 inside its 10 mm body; the hub cutter ends at Z=7 inside its 8 mm body. Their bolt passages retain 2 mm and 1 mm roofs. These solids still pass watertight-mesh checks.

    Retire these interfaces with the wheel design. For any reused part, test a full fastener/tool insertion path against actual CSG and measured hardware.

  6. ARCH-01 — A ten-stitch comb does not locate an entire round

    A roughly 100-stitch round exceeds the ten comb positions. V1 has no demonstrated transfer sequence that keeps each future insertion point located until the next round reaches it. A mesh or timed animation cannot fill this gap.

    Replace passive reacquisition with retained stitch positions. Prove insertion, loop formation and transfer on a short needle bed before designing circular storage.

  7. YARN-02 — Hook closure and yarn retraction are missing control functions

    The V1 hook is permanently open. Its dancer is a passive arm; there is no measured yarn-tension loop or commanded slack take-up. Their adequacy for repeated draw-through is an untested assumption.

    Evaluate a polished metal compound tool with controlled closure and rotation, plus measured feeding/retraction. Establish tool/yarn compatibility physically before freezing dimensions.

  8. EVID-01 — Existing odds and cycle times are not experimental results

    The prior one-percent completion figure is a product of subjective stage probabilities. Timing is programmed into a simulation. Neither is a measured chance or speed of this machine.

    Record trial counts, failure modes, actual cycle times and recovery outcomes. Report assumptions explicitly; do not use simulated timing or invented odds as a purchasing argument.

  9. MECH-03 — Wheel retention and bearings need a complete design

    The existing tooth and wheel hub have no shared mounting contract. Shaft supports, positive torque transfer, pulley geometry and belt tensioning are not fully modelled.

    Specify bought shaft/bearings/pulleys, CAD each support and retainer, then check the swept volume against the comb.

  10. MECH-04 — Linear drives and yarn-over actuator are incomplete

    The legacy hardware picture uses rail/motor envelopes. It does not contain a complete Z/R/P bearing, lead-nut, coupling, endstop and carriage attachment chain. The yarn finger also needs its actuator and mount.

    Freeze exact supplier drawings and model every load-bearing interface before issuing assembly instructions.

  11. CTRL-01 — No deployable machine controller exists

    HKP/1 is a protocol and simulated transport. The repository does not provide tested ESP32 motion firmware, a complete wiring drawing, homing, or an integrated detector.

    Implement and bench-test homing, travel limits, sensor faults, stop/reset behaviour and one measured stitch cycle. Size the DC power system from the selected loads.

  12. YARN-01 — Automatic crochet has no physical evidence

    Rendered motion and watertight meshes do not demonstrate stitch capture, loop retention, draw-through, round transfer, increases or colour changes.

    Record fit trials, then a single stitch, 50 consecutive stitches, a closed round, increases, one complete single-colour hat, and finally colour changes.

  13. MFG-01 — X1 Carbon selected; manufacturing profile is not qualified

    The owner confirmed Bambu Lab X1 Carbon. A 0.4 mm nozzle is assumed, not confirmed. Material brand, shrinkage, supports and physical fit remain unqualified. The 256 mm bed also has a front-left exclusion in its default profile.

    Slice and inspect each plate using explicit profiles and retain support-inclusive grams/time. Check fit physically before releasing mating parts. Slicing old CAD does not resolve its mechanical defects.

Costs and print handoff

No defensible complete-machine grams, print hours or build total exists for the selected redesign. The old architecture's priced purchase lines total 9,379 NOK, largely from historical allowances, with 12 unpriced categories. That is not the redesign's cost.

The optional old-geometry comparison kit is approximately 37.6 g by a rough CAD shell/infill calculation. It excludes support, brim, purge and failed prints. Its printer-ready quote requires the friend's actual printer profile, plate quantities, slicer grams/hours and charging rates. See the optional kit guide. The 3MF contains geometry only.

Primary evidence and its limits

  • CroMat doctoral research · Storck, 2024

    Paired auxiliary needles retain the fabric. The tool has controlled shaft/slider motion and rotation. Printed auxiliary bars were rejected after friction and bending problems. The documented prototype uses ten axes; its trials do not validate our DK-cotton hat.

    Sections 3.1.4–3.1.5 and 3.4; printed pp. 52–56, 89–94, 100–101. Full text inspected.

    Our design inference: Use this as a mechanism reference, not as a drop-in ten-axis parts list or a promise that a simpler clone will work.

  • Machine-producible crochet patterns · Storck et al., 2023

    The flat-bed process has specific shaping limits, including where increases and decreases can occur. Circular hand patterns cannot simply be sent to a flat-bed controller.

    Introduction and pp. 265–267; paper and stitch-topology figure inspected.

    Our design inference: A panel-built hat needs a separate pattern compiler and changes the construction. It is not silently interchangeable with the current hat.

  • Active low-cost thread tensioning · Kellner et al., 2025

    This CroMat project describes active feeding/retraction with a storage spring and load-cell feedback.

    Author conference abstract, published 3 December 2025. Not a production reliability report.

    Our design inference: Measure tension through the whole stitch cycle and design slack management explicitly.

  • Loom-Based Mechanized Crochet · Smith et al., 2026

    The published abstract describes a robotic tool acting on a constrained fabric matrix.

    Abstract only; SCF 2025 event, publication dated 18 February 2026. Full method and performance not verified.

    Our design inference: Additional support for investigating constrained fabric. No conclusion about affordable home-scale hat production follows from this abstract.
