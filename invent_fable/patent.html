Automated crochet apparatus with stitch-presentation wheel for spiral single-crochet fabrication

"HEKLOMAT-1" — an apparatus and method for machine-forming true crocheted fabric (fastmaske / single crochet) in a continuous top-down spiral with multi-strand tapestry colorwork, from a compiled per-stitch instruction stream.

Title: Automated crochet apparatus with stitch-presentation wheel Applicant: Espen Horne Classification (est.): D04B 33/00 · D05B · B33Y 80/00 Status: Unfiled evaluation draft — invent_fable dossier Figures: 7 Claims: 18 (4 independent)

1. Technical field

The invention relates to textile fabrication machinery, and specifically to an apparatus and method for the automated production of crocheted fabric — interlooped stitches formed one at a time by a single hook — in tubular and three-dimensional shapes such as bucket hats, including multi-color tapestry crochet, from a digital per-stitch program.

2. Background and prior art

Weaving and knitting were mechanized in the 18th and 19th centuries; crochet never was. The commercial machines marketed as "crochet machines" (crochet galloon machines) are in fact warp-knitting machines and do not form crochet stitches. The obstacle is well understood: a single crochet stitch requires inserting a hook through a specific loop of the previous row, wrapping yarn over the hook, and drawing it through held loops — a sequence in which the target loop must be located, held open, and oriented before the hook arrives, while every other loop in the fabric is relaxed and closed.

Research prototypes confirm both the difficulty and the path forward:

  • Croche-Matic (G. Perry, J. L. García del Castillo y López, N. Melenbrink, Harvard GSD; IEEE ICRA 2023): a nine-axis radial machine built around the magic-ring technique, producing chain and single-crochet stitches, increases and decreases in the round. Reported stitch success was roughly one in two, with at most four consecutive stitches — the dominant failure being loop localization and retention at the moment of hook insertion.
  • CroMat (Bielefeld University of Applied Sciences / TU Dresden; patented, Storck dissertation): the first crochet automaton producing chain, slip, single and half-double crochet stitches with turns, increases and decreases — in flat crochet only. Its key teaching: a straight row of latch needles holds the previous row's loops open, so the hook never searches for a loop.
  • Loom-based mechanized crochet (N. Smith, ACM SCF 2025); robotic-arm crochet toolpaths (Technion, CAADRIA 2023); US 12,497,720 and US 12,454,777 (crochet assistance apparatus, hand-guided).

No known apparatus combines (a) crochet worked in the round, (b) continuous spiral construction with increases, (c) multi-strand tapestry colorwork, and (d) closed-loop stitch verification — the combination required to produce garments such as crocheted bucket hats. The present invention provides that combination, and does so with a mechanism count far below Croche-Matic's nine axes per stitch.

3. Summary of the invention

The invention rests on one enabling insight: the previous round's stitches need not be found — they can be delivered. A stitch-presentation wheel (20) — a small indexing wheel whose teeth are standard knitting-machine latch needles (22) — engages the top loops ("the V") of the previous round like a film sprocket engages perforations: each tooth picks up one loop ahead of the working zone, carries it to a fixed work point where the latch holds it open at a known position and orientation, and releases it behind the working zone. Loop localization thereby becomes a deterministic mechanical property of the machine rather than a sensing problem. The wheel is diameter-independent: the same eight-tooth wheel serves a 10-stitch magic ring and a 144-stitch brim. An increase is two hook cycles on one held loop; the fabric registration cannot drift because the wheel is geared to the fabric pitch itself.

Around the wheel, the apparatus comprises: a turntable (14) carrying an exchangeable 3D-printed mandrel (16) whose surface of revolution equals the computed hat profile; a hook module (30) holding an unmodified 4 mm crochet hook (32) with plunge, lift and twist axes; a servo-driven yarn-over finger (40) that wraps the active strand over the hook; a six-station yarn carousel (50) of which up to three strands are active per round, the inactive strands being laid over the presentation wheel so that each stitch is formed around them — exactly the hand technique of tapestry crochet; a spring tension arm (54) holding 15–25 g of strand tension; and a controller (60) executing a compiled per-stitch instruction stream ("Maskekode": MR, FM, INC, COL n, SLST, FO) with a camera (64) verifying each presented loop before hook insertion. The identical instruction stream drives a previsualization simulator — machine and simulation are digital twins by construction.

5. Detailed description

5.1 Work holding (12, 14, 16)

An aluminium-extrusion frame 12 carries a rim-driven turntable 14 (the C-axis) on a lazy-susan ring bearing. The turntable mounts an exchangeable mandrel 16, 3D-printed in three bayonet-coupled sections (crown dome, wall, brim skirt) whose outer surface is the surface of revolution of the target hat's computed profile — the same ring-by-ring profile the pattern software uses. The fabric 18 therefore drapes onto its final geometry as it is formed, giving constant, known loop positions at the free working edge. Interchangeable mandrels cover head sizes 52–60 cm.

5.2 The stitch-presentation wheel (20, 22, 24, 26) — FIG. 2

The wheel core 20 is a printed hub carrying eight standard latch needles 22 as radial teeth, retained by set screws and a cover. The wheel turns about an axis radial to the fabric, on an arm 26 whose slotted mount sets mesh depth. Its rotation is electronically geared 1:1 to the stitch pitch: each 2π/8 index advances one tooth into the pick-up zone, where the tooth's open hook enters the approaching top loop; fabric tension rides the loop up the needle shank, opening the latch 24. At the work point the loop is held open at a fixed spatial position. After the hook module has formed the new stitch through it, continued rotation carries the loop into the release zone where it slides off, closing the latch. Because engagement is positive (sprocket-like), registration cannot accumulate error at any round count, and an increase (INC) is executed by simply commanding two hook cycles before the next index.

5.3 Hook module (30–36) — FIG. 3

An unmodified 4.0 mm aluminium crochet hook 32 is clamped in a printed collet on a carriage riding a vertical MGN7 rail (plunge P), itself mounted on a cross-arm carriage (radial R) and a column carriage (height Z); a small stepper twists the hook (T) to present its throat to the yarn. R and Z track the current round's radius and height (FIG. 4), so the plunge stroke is a constant 18 mm regardless of where on the hat the machine is working. The stitch cycle of FIG. 3 uses at most ten coordinated motions — compared with ~30 reported for the prior-art radial machine — because presentation removes all searching and holding motions.

5.4 Yarn system (40, 50–56) — FIG. 5

A servo finger 40 wraps the active strand over the hook twice per stitch. Up to six spools ride the carousel 50; the compiler limits active strands to three per round (the published patterns' own constraint). Inactive strands route through a common eyelet so they lie along the presented loops and are enclosed by each stitch — machine tapestry crochet. A COL n op rotates the carousel and schedules the strand swap into the final pull-through of the preceding stitch, the exact hand technique, costing 4 s. A spring arm 54 with adjustable preload holds strand tension at 15–25 g.

5.5 Control and verification (60–64, 70) — FIG. 6, 7

A Raspberry Pi 60 compiles pattern data to the Maskekode stream and streams motion segments to a 3D-printer-class stepper board 62. A macro camera 64 aimed at the work point verifies each presented loop before hook insertion; on failure the wheel re-indexes and retries, and after three failures the machine pauses with the working loop safe on the hook — the machine's state is always recoverable because crochet has exactly one live loop. The identical opcode stream drives the browser previsualization; cycle-time constants measured on the prototype update both executors simultaneously.

5.6 Manufacture

All 21 bespoke components are FDM-printable on a 256 mm-class printer (validated watertight, largest part ⌀240 mm); all other components are commodity items (NEMA17 steppers, MGN7 rail, GT2 belts, lazy-susan bearing, MG90S servos, latch needles, Raspberry Pi, SKR-class board). Complete bill of materials ≈ 5 000 NOK excluding printer and filament (~650 g).

7. Abstract

An apparatus for automated crochet in the round. A stitch-presentation wheel with latch-needle teeth engages the top loops of the previous round like a sprocket, presenting each loop held open at a fixed work point where a standard crochet hook, a servo yarn-over finger and a multi-station yarn carousel form true single-crochet stitches in a continuous spiral over a 3D-printed mandrel, including tapestry colorwork by crocheting over carried strands. Loop localization — the failure mode of prior crochet automation — becomes a deterministic mechanical property. A compiler converts per-stitch pattern data into an instruction stream executed identically by the machine and by a 3D simulator, so fabrication time and appearance are known in advance. All bespoke parts print on a 256 mm FDM printer; remaining components are commodity motion hardware totaling roughly €430.
